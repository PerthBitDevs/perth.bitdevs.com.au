Quantum attacks would target signing keys

A private key creates a signature authorising a spend. The corresponding public key lets everyone verify that signature.

WHAT WOULD BREAK?
  • Bitcoin relies on it being impractical to calculate the private key from the public key.
  • Shor’s algorithm is a quantum computation that could do this on a sufficiently capable machine.
  • An attacker could then sign a competing spend without stealing the owner’s device or backup.
WHY PUBLIC-KEY EXPOSURE MATTERS
  • Exposing a public key is normal and does not reveal the private key to ordinary computers.
  • Some Bitcoin outputs hide the public key behind a hash until spending. Taproot outputs expose a public key from creation.
  • Post-quantum signatures use different mathematical problems intended to resist known quantum attacks.

The research on the next slide improves parts of a possible attack. Neither result demonstrates recovery of a Bitcoin private key.

More reliable quantum operations

Qubits store quantum information. Many physical qubits can encode one more reliable logical qubit through error correction.

GOOGLE: PREPARE RELIABLE HELPERS
  • Some required operations use up specially prepared quantum states called magic states. Preparing them reliably is expensive.
  • Google’s Willow processor reduced their error about 40× compared with initial preparation alone, while retaining 8% of attempts.
ANTHROPIC: USE FEWER OPERATIONS
  • A quantum circuit is a sequence of operations. These circuits simplify one arithmetic step used in key recovery: adding elliptic-curve points.
  • One uses 17× fewer Toffoli gates, a costly operation, with 12% more logical qubits than the previous lowest-qubit circuit for that step.

The 17× figure is not an end-to-end attack speedup. Google’s 15 October report describes work first posted in December 2025.

Quantum-safe signing and wallet backups

Coinbase’s 13 October proposal sets requirements for new signature schemes used by wallets and custodians.

THE BACKUP PROBLEM
  • Some hash-based signatures use each piece of signing material once. Wallets must record which pieces they used.
  • Restoring an old backup can roll back that record. Reusing signing material can enable forged signatures.
  • Coinbase asks for a stateless fallback whose safety does not depend on tracking earlier uses.
SHARED CUSTODY AND BITCOIN RULES
  • Multi-party computation (MPC) lets custody systems jointly sign using shares of a key, splitting signing authority.
  • New schemes need to support that arrangement. Splitting keys does not by itself make the signature quantum-resistant.
  • Also required: upgradeable output formats and measurements of signature size and node verification costs.

A migration that allows either an old or a post-quantum signature still permits a quantum attacker to use the old route while it remains enabled.

Spending can expose keys for other coins

Zeeshan Ali Khan’s 19 October proposal moves spendable coins together when their shared public-key material becomes exposed.

EXAMPLE: REUSING AN ADDRESS
  • A common SegWit address type, P2WPKH, stores a hash of the public key. Spending reveals the key for signature verification.
  • Receive 0.2 BTC and 0.3 BTC at the same address. Spending the 0.2 BTC also reveals the key controlling the unspent 0.3 BTC.
  • A future quantum attacker could target that remaining coin.
PROPOSED WALLET BEHAVIOUR
  • Before revealing key material outside the trusted signing setup, prepare spends for every newly exposed, confirmed coin it can spend.
  • Broadcast together and send change to outputs whose required public-key material remains hidden.
  • This is an opt-in wallet policy, without a Bitcoin consensus change.

It does not stop an attack before confirmation or guarantee the transactions confirm together. The wallet needs to know earlier disclosures; grouping coins can link them publicly.

A bond for spending vulnerable coins

After quantum key recovery becomes possible, a signature alone cannot distinguish the old owner from someone who recovered the key.

OWNER PREPARES A HIDDEN PROOF
  • Cory Fields’ 4 November “quantum landmines” idea starts before keys can be broken.
  • The owner signs a binding record of a new post-quantum key, called a commitment, using the old key. They hide the proof in a quantum-safe output.
  • Revealing that earlier proof can later establish prior control of the old key.
EXAMPLE: AN ATTACKER SPENDS 1 BTC
  • The proposed rules require another 1 BTC as collateral. Both coins remain locked during a challenge period.
  • The owner reveals the prepared proof and claims both coins, costing the attacker their collateral.
  • Without a challenge, the spender receives the coins after the timeout.

This is a thought experiment needing new Bitcoin rules. Legitimate owners also need collateral; lost keys cannot prepare proofs. Deployment mechanics remain open.
